Pixel Magnifier

Low vision users rely on their phone's camera to read the world — menus, medication labels, mail. Pixel had a magnifier app. It just wasn't good enough. We rebuilt it from the ground up around how low vision users actually hold and use their phones — and shipped the highest-rated accessibility app on Google Play.

The highest-rated app on Google Play — transforming a forgotten accessibility utility into a tool that millions of low-vision users rely on every day, earning recognition from leading accessibility organizations worldwide.

Lead UX Designer

The Challenge

People with low vision rely heavily on their phone's camera to magnify everyday things like menus and bills. While functional, it is a makeshift solution. Our competitor, iPhone, had a dedicated Magnifier app, and Pixel needed to provide an equally powerful, accessible, and intuitive tool — one that could go beyond the competition.

Discovery & Research

Our research involved competitive analysis, institutional partnerships with organizations like the Royal Institute for the Blind, and extensive user interviews. The most surprising insight came from direct observation: due to their low vision, users hold the phone extremely close to their faces, often with screen magnification at maximum. Moving from one edge of the screen to the other could require multiple swipes, making feature discovery a fundamental challenge.

This screenshot from a research session shows the "close-up perspective" of a low-vision user with screen magnification at maximum. This insight was critical in our decision to cluster all UI controls.

Design & Iteration

The "close-up perspective" insight completely changed our design approach. We initially explored placing controls across the screen, similar to the main Camera app. Testing showed this was unusable for our target audience. We pivoted to a design that clustered all core functionality in a small, predictable area at the bottom of the screen. While it might look "unbalanced" to a normally-sighted person, it proved far more accessible for low-vision users.

The original Magnifier gave prime real estate to "Reset zoom" — a low-frequency action — while scattering higher-value controls across the full width of the screen. For a user with system magnification at maximum, the settings icon and gallery icon were effectively on opposite ends of the world.

Concept A — Rejected

Controls clustered at the bottom, but testing revealed users still struggled with the menu and small icon sizes.

Concept B — Rejected

Improved icon sizes, but still required too much horizontal screen travel for users with extreme magnification.

Final Design

All controls clustered at the bottom in a minimal, predictable zone. The most accessible direction — and the chosen path.

Designing Against Assumptions

The bottom-clustered layout was the right solution for our users. It was not an easy sell internally.

Google has strong design system standards — expressive typography, visual curves, and a preference for the top app bar as the home for overflow controls. These conventions exist for good reasons and work well across most Android apps. Our research showed they didn't work for Magnifier's users.

Low vision users hold the phone inches from their face, often with system-wide magnification already at maximum. At that distance, a button at the top of the screen isn't just inconvenient — it requires physically traversing the entire display, multiple swipes just to reach a control. Distributing UI elements across the screen the way a standard Android app might was effectively hiding features from the people who needed them most.

The argument that moved leadership wasn't a design rationale. It was video. We brought session recordings that showed, concretely, how close users hold the phone — the physical reality of interacting with a screen at 2× system magnification. Seeing it made the abstract argument unnecessary. The bottom-clustered layout wasn't a deviation from good design. It was what good design looked like for this user.

Live Search

Solving the layout got low vision users to the controls. It didn't solve what they were trying to do once they got there.

The core task — scanning a dense real-world document like a restaurant menu or a prescription label — still required physically sweeping the phone across the page, line by line. We couldn't reformat the menu. We couldn't reorganize the grocery aisle. But we could eliminate the scanning problem entirely.

Live Search lets users type any word — a dish name, a price, an ingredient — and Magnifier instantly highlights it in the camera view. The physical world stays exactly as it is; the app finds what you're looking for within it.

The design challenge wasn't just the feature — it was the interaction pattern. Search behavior varies across Android apps, and we didn't want users to have to learn a new paradigm on top of everything else. We audited search across Google's own ecosystem and landed on YouTube's pattern as the closest model: familiar to a wide audience, optimized for quick repeated lookups, and adaptable to Magnifier's one-handed use context.

The response from accessibility organizations — and from users — was immediate. One user expressed excitement about the live search:

"Found it right away. And how easy could that be? That, I mean, that is awesome. That is convenient."

User Research Participant

The final Live Search feature in action — users type any word and Magnifier highlights it instantly in the camera view, eliminating the need to manually scan.

Solution & Impact

The final app provides powerful magnification, filters, and a unique live search feature — all accessible from a simple, consolidated control panel. The launch was a milestone for Pixel's accessibility commitment.

What I'd Take Forward

Magnifier taught me that the most important accessibility research happens before you open Figma. The close-up perspective insight — watching how users physically hold the phone — changed the entire design direction. No amount of pattern library review would have surfaced it.

That kind of grounded, user-led process is what I bring to consulting engagements. If you're building products for disabled users or older adults and want a collaborator who's done this work at scale, I'd like to hear about what you're working on.
